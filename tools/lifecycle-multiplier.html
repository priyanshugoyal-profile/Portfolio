A story about one sentence

The most expensive word in your message

Here is a line somebody writes in a lending company every single day. It takes about nine seconds.

The nine-second sentence

“Apply now — get an instant loan approved in 10 minutes. Guaranteed approval, no documents needed. Offer expires tonight!”

Four things just happened, and nobody in the room knows about any of them yet.

The WhatsApp bill went up seven times

The word “offer” turned it into an advertisement. WhatsApp charges about seven times more to deliver an advertisement than an update about something a customer already started.

One text message became three

That long dash is not on a phone keypad. One character outside the basic set and the whole message switches to an alphabet that fits less than half as much in.

Nearly a third of people will never see it

An advertisement cannot be sent by text to anyone who asked not to be advertised at. An update about their own loan can. Same people, same phone, different rules.

It breaks Reserve Bank rules three times over

A loan in ten minutes. A guaranteed yes. No documents. The rulebook for lenders in India has something to say about all three, and it is not subtle.

Nothing on the screen went red. It never does. The bill arrives at the end of the month, the compliance email arrives the month after, and by then nobody remembers who typed the word “offer”.

So I built the thing that goes red.

What this does

You write one message. It writes that message four ways — WhatsApp, text, email and a phone notification — and then, for each one, it tells you two things almost nobody puts on the same screen:

What it will cost, worked out the way the phone networks and WhatsApp actually work it out. And whether you are allowed to say it, checked against the rules Indian lenders live under.

Then it shows you the same message written properly, so you can see the difference in rupees and in risk at the same time.

Step three

Why the same sentence has two prices

This is the part that surprises people, so it is worth going slowly.

WhatsApp has two price lists, and your words decide which one you are on

WhatsApp sorts every business message into buckets. The two that matter here are:

An update — something about a thing this person already started. Their application. Their instalment. A document they still owe you. WhatsApp calls this a utility message and charges very little for it, because it is genuinely useful and nobody asked to be sold to.

An advertisement — an offer, an invitation, anything trying to start something new. WhatsApp calls this marketing and charges roughly seven and a half times as much.

The trap

You cannot talk your way into the cheap bucket. The relationship decides the bucket, not the wording. If this person has no application open with you, there is nothing to update them about, and any message you send is an advertisement no matter how you phrase it.

But the reverse absolutely happens, and it is expensive. Somebody writes a perfectly good update about a pending application, then adds one cheerful sentence at the end — “and you could get up to ₹15 lakh!” — and that single sentence drags the whole message into the expensive bucket, on every send, forever.

The tool above spots that sentence and tells you what it costs.

Text messages have a cheap alphabet and an expensive one

Phones have two ways of storing a text message. The cheap way handles the ordinary characters on a keyboard and fits 160 of them into one message. The expensive way handles everything else — curly quotes, long dashes, the rupee sign, emoji — and only fits 70.

One unusual character anywhere switches the whole message to the expensive way. A perfectly normal 140-character text suddenly needs three messages instead of one, and your send costs three times as much. Nothing warns you, because to a human the two apostrophes look identical.

And then there is who you are allowed to text

In India a text message is registered as either an advertisement or a service message before it is ever sent. An advertisement cannot go to anybody who has signed up to stop receiving advertisements, and it can only be sent between nine in the morning and nine at night.

A service message — a real update about something they already have with you — reaches everybody, at any hour. It costs a little more per message and reaches considerably more people. On a base of any size the cheaper message is usually the more expensive decision.

Where these prices come from

WhatsApp publishes its own rates, and in India an advertisement currently costs ₹0.8631 to deliver against ₹0.1150 for an update. On top of that sits whatever your messaging supplier charges, usually five to thirty paise, and 18% tax on the lot. Both of those are adjustable below, because yours will differ.

Text messages run roughly ₹0.08–₹0.12 as an advertisement and ₹0.10–₹0.18 as a service message, per part. Email is a few paise. A phone notification is free to send and reaches only people who installed the app and left notifications on, which is never as many as anybody hopes.

Rates move. That is rather the point of putting them in a box you can edit rather than baking them into a slide.

The one people find hardest

You may not say a loan will be approved. Not “guaranteed”, not “assured”, not “100%”. Every marketer's instinct is to remove doubt, and in lending the doubt is the honest part — a credit check has not happened yet.

What you can do is be precise about the thing that is genuinely fast. Checking whether somebody pre-qualifies really can take under a minute. Money in the account cannot. “Check in a minute” is true. “Loan in a minute” is a rule broken. One word apart, and the whole difference is whether you are describing the check or the cash.

Why the boring half is the valuable half

Plenty of things will write you four versions of a message. Almost nothing checks them against the price list of the place they are going, and nothing at all checks them against the rulebook of the industry you are in.

So the copy arrives looking finished, costs four times what it needed to, and somebody in compliance finds out about it six weeks later. The writing was never the bottleneck.
