A story about guessing

Which ad worked — and more to the point, why?

Your ad platform can tell you that ad number 17 did well. It cannot tell you whether that was the price, the photo, or the line about not needing a cosigner. So next month, you guess again.

The report that doesn't help

You ran 38 ads last month. The platform ranks them neatly and shows you the winners. Useful, for about five minutes.

Because the question you actually have is what do I make next week? And for that, “ad 17 won” tells you nothing. Was it the discount? The face in the picture? The promise about no paperwork? You cannot tell from the report, so you make 38 more ads and find out all over again.

There is a second trap underneath the first. The ad at the top of your list might have had four sales. Four sales is not proof of anything. Most reports will happily let you pour next quarter's budget into a coin toss.

Step 3 · So which idea actually won?

This is the chart your ad platform will never show you. Not which ad won — which idea won, with every ad that used it added together.

First, a quick word about luck. Flip a coin four times, get three heads, and you have not found a magic coin. You have found four coin flips.

Ads are the same. An idea with four sales behind it might be genuinely brilliant, or it might be four lucky clicks. Most reports will not tell you which — they just sort by the biggest number and let you assume.

This one draws the line out loud. Solid bars are results with enough behind them to act on. Striped, faded bars are results that could easily just be luck. The little line through each bar shows the range the true answer could sit in — if that line crosses your average, the idea is not really beating anything. You can still look. You just cannot accidentally bet the budget on a coin toss.

Step 6 · What if you moved the money?

Drag the slider to take budget away from the ideas that are genuinely losing money and put it behind the best one. Only ideas with solid evidence are allowed to move — the tool will not let you shuffle budget based on a hunch.

What this tool actually does

Four steps, none of them mysterious. The only clever part is the bit that refuses to give you an answer when there is not enough evidence for one.

1
Does the sums

Works out, for every ad, what you spent, what came back, and what each sale cost you.

2
Reads the headlines

Labels each ad by the argument it makes and the way it opens, using a word list you can read in full on this page.

3
Groups them by idea

Adds up every ad that made the same argument. Thirty-eight noisy rows become six clear choices.

4
Checks whether it is real

Asks how much is riding on each result. Anything that could plausibly be luck gets labelled as such rather than dressed up as a finding.

5
Does the what-if honestly

Models moving the budget, and prints the assumption it made right next to the answer instead of hiding it in a footnote.

6
Tells you what to do

What to try next, what to switch off — built only from the results that held up.

The bit I would want you to notice. It would have been easier, and honestly more impressive-looking, to rank the six ideas one to six and call it a day. Charts like that get made every week. But two of those six results are not strong enough to act on, and a chart that hides that is worse than no chart — it gets a real budget moved on the strength of four lucky sales. Saying “not enough data yet” out loud is the whole point of the thing.
